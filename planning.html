Planning and retirement

Will there be enough, and when?

That is the question a register cannot answer. WealthHorizon has two planners: a guided one that asks only the questions that apply to you, and an advanced one where you set every assumption yourself. They share an engine, so an answer you give the first is an input the second already has. Both are in the Pro edition.

Every screen on this page is the running application with a demonstration household loaded. The figures are that household’s, and they are fictitious.

Start guided

You pick the kind of plan you are making, answer questions in your own words, and get a result. It fills in what it can from your accounts before it asks, so you are checking figures rather than looking them up.

Good when you do not yet know what the important assumptions are.

Or set every assumption

Return rate, inflation, retirement age, the order you spend your pots in, each pension and its commencement age, each income stream and when it stops. Nothing is decided for you and nothing is hidden.

Good when you know exactly what you want to model, and want to see it change.

They are the same plan. Answer the guided questions and the Advanced Planner button carries your answers across; go the other way and the guided results read from the plan you built by hand. There is no second set of figures to keep in step, which is the usual reason a plan and a spreadsheet stop agreeing.

The guided planner

Six starting points, and only the questions that apply

Most planning tools open on a blank form and ask for a retirement age. That is the hardest question to answer first. This one starts by asking what kind of plan you are making, and the questions change to suit.

It asks only what applies to you

The questions are conditional. No children, and it never asks about school fees. Children over eighteen, and it asks about university support instead. Say you have no investments and the detailed investment questions do not appear at all.

It fills in what it already knows

Your cash, ISA, general investment account, pensions, crypto and property figures, your state and defined-benefit pensions, your salary and contributions, and your age come from the accounts you already keep. You check and adjust rather than dig out statements — and because they came from the register, they stay right.

The health and burnout questions are optional

They exist because longevity and care risk change a plan materially, and they are marked as answer-only-if-you-are-comfortable. If you share your plan with an adviser they see those answers too, which is stated before you answer rather than after.

The advanced planner

Every assumption is yours, and every one is visible

The same engine with the guardrails off. Build as many plans as you like, name them, export them as files, and compare them.

Tell it what you intend to spend, and the projection changes shape. With no target it draws a fixed percentage of the pot each year, which illustrates growth and cannot tell you when the money runs out. Enter a target spend and it works out what your guaranteed income — state pension, defined benefit, earnings — leaves uncovered, and draws exactly that, grossed up for the tax you will pay on it. It then reports the first year you fall short, when the pension is exhausted, when all liquid assets are, and what is left at the end.

FIRE

The number, the date, and the gap in the middle

Choose a FIRE-style journey and the planner adds the measures that community actually uses — and the one that gets skipped, which is how you live between stopping work and reaching pension access age.

  • Freedom numberThe capital that makes work optional, at the withdrawal rate you chose rather than a rate the tool picked. Shown as both the figure and the multiple of spending it represents.
  • FI progressWhat you have against what you need, as a percentage — and above 100% it keeps counting rather than stopping at “done”, because how far past the line you are is the interesting part.
  • Years to freedomAnd the work-optional age that follows from it, on your current savings rate.
  • Savings rateOf take-home pay, counting pension contributions and employer contributions as well as what reaches a savings account — the three together, because leaving out the pension is how this number gets flattered.
  • Coast FIREWhether what you already hold will grow into the freedom number without another contribution, and the coast number as it stands today.
  • Bridge gapThe one that gets skipped. Stop work at 52 and you cannot touch a pension until 57 or 58 — the classic pension-rich, access-poor position. This states the gap in years and the money it needs, from the accessible side of your balance sheet only.
  • Essential coverHow much of your essential spending guaranteed income covers on its own, which is a different and more comforting question than whether the pot is big enough.
  • Lifestyle ladderLean, Base and Fat budgets side by side, so the number moves with the life rather than being one figure you have to believe.
  • Plan resilienceA blended, deliberately approximate read across progress, bridge funding, savings rate and cash buffer. It is a prompt, not a score to optimise, and it says so.
  • MilestonesEmergency fund in place, debt-free except the mortgage, a tenth of the way, a quarter — the markers that make a twenty-year project feel like it has steps.

There is a plain-English / adviser-terms switch at the top of the page. The same figures, labelled either the way the FIRE community says them or the way a suitability report has to. Neither is a simplification of the other.

Goals, and tracking against them

What you are saving for, and whether you are actually on course

A goal is easy to write down and hard to keep honest. These are funded from the money you actually have, in the order the dates fall, and the answer changes when your accounts do.

In today’s money, on purpose

You say what the thing costs now. Inflating it to the target year is the tool’s job, not yours, and doing it in your head is where these estimates usually go wrong.

Priority decides what gives

Must-have, important, nice-to-have. When there is not enough to go round, a must-have that looks short is flagged rather than quietly funded last, and the nice-to-haves are what the suggestions offer to trim.

It says what it ignores

Tax, currency conversion and borrowing are not modelled here. It is a guide for deciding which goals to prioritise, and it says so on the page rather than in a footnote you have to find.

Plan against actual

Capture a snapshot and the roadmap starts a tracking line: where the plan said you would be by now, against where you are. A plan nobody checks against reality is a wish, and this is the check.

Four ways to close a gap

Where a goal is short, the suggestions are quantified rather than encouraging: save this much more each month, delay it by this long, trim it to what is affordable, or reprioritise so the must-haves come first.

Accessible or locked

Every figure separates what you can reach from what you cannot. A deposit needed in three years cannot come out of a pension, and a total that mixes the two answers the wrong question.

What a projection is

None of this is advice, and the page says so where you read it

Everything here is a projection from assumptions you entered. Markets move, tax rules change, and the further out a line goes the less it is worth. A banner says exactly that at the top of the planner rather than in small print at the bottom.

Where a figure depends on a judgement rather than arithmetic, the judgement is yours and the tool shows its working. The order you spend your pots in is the clearest example: the conventional sequence is cash, then investments, then ISAs, then pensions — but where unused pension funds are exposed to inheritance tax and other holdings are not, spending the pension first can be right. WealthHorizon will model either and tell you what each costs. It will not tell you which to pick, and for a cross-border position you should take advice.

The Monte Carlo simulation is there for the same reason: a single line implies a precision that does not exist, and a range of outcomes is the more honest picture.
