The shape of the market

Four kinds of product, and which one this is

Whatever you buy, it will be one of these four. No brands are named. The trade-off comes with the kind of product, not the brand, and the trade-off is the part worth thinking about.

Desktop software you install

You buy it. It runs on your machine. The file is yours, and it keeps working when the vendor has a bad year.

The cost is that it is tied to that machine and that operating system. Somebody has to keep it up to date.

A cloud subscription

Somebody else runs it. There is nothing to install and it works on every device. Bank feeds tend to be better, because the vendor looks after them.

The cost is that your records sit on their servers, on their terms, for as long as you keep paying.

Open source you host

Free, and nobody can take it away. Usually a ledger or a budgeting tool, built well by people who wanted one themselves.

The cost is that you are the support desk. These tools also rarely go beyond recording what has already happened.

Adviser platforms

What an advice firm buys. A back office to hold the clients. A cashflow tool to do the planning. Something else to produce the compliance reporting.

The cost is that this is usually three or four products, bought separately and wired together.

WealthHorizon is the third kind, aiming at what the first and fourth do. You install every edition on your own hardware, so your records never leave your building. It goes past recording what happened, into planning, tax and inheritance tax. And the Advisory edition holds the client book, the planning and the Consumer Duty evidence in one place instead of three. The question is whether that is worth running it yourself. No is a fair answer.

What WealthHorizon does not do

These are reasons to buy something else. You can check every one against this site or against the product. We could not honestly say that about anybody else's software.

  • Bank feeds are not in production The Open Banking integration is built, and it works against the provider's sandbox. To use it for real we need regulatory permissions and provider onboarding, the same as any account information service. We do not have them yet. So if you want transactions to arrive on their own today, this is the wrong product. It is on the limitations list too.
  • One broker connection, and it is read-only Trading 212 is built and you can connect it. Saxo is still at design stage. Nothing else exists. Everything else arrives by file import. The application imports a lot of formats and does it well, but you are still the one doing it.
  • No integrations with adviser platforms There is no link to any back-office system, platform or provider feed. A firm with hundreds of clients should assume data arrives by import, and should price that work in before anything else.
  • You have to run it Every edition installs on your own hardware. That is the point, and it is also a cost. Somebody has to install it, back it up, patch it and keep the machine alive. We will not take that over for you. The moment we hold your records, the main reason to use this has gone.
  • The planner does not model everything It is not a general-purpose modeller, on purpose. It will not tell you which option to take. It holds every rate and allowance as reference data, so it never guesses at a case it has no rules for. And it says outright that cross-border pension and inheritance tax questions need a qualified specialist. What it does model is set out in full.
  • No independent accessibility audit We build to the WCAG 2.2 criteria for keyboard use, visible focus and target size, and we test them in the browser suite. But we have not paid for an outside audit, and we claim no formal conformance level. If your procurement needs one, ask us first.
  • No native mobile application There is no iPhone or Android app. The web application is built for phones and tablets, and it reads offline once you have looked at something. It is still a web application.
  • It is new, and we are small Muneris Management Ltd is a small company, and WealthHorizon has not been on the market for years. Maybe you want an established vendor with a support desk, a user group and a published roadmap. If so, that is a good reason to buy from one.

If you are comparing

The questions worth asking any of them

Including us. These are the questions where the answer tends to decide it. They are also the ones where the answer is rarely on the front page.

  • Where do my records physically live, and what happens to them if I stop paying?
  • Can I get everything out, in a format something else can read, without asking anyone?
  • Which countries does it actually work in? Some well-known products are sold only in North America. Bank connections are usually country by country as well.
  • More than one currency, properly? Not just a display setting. Does each entry keep the rate and date it was converted at, so last year's figures do not move when today's rate does?
  • More than one tax jurisdiction in the same household?
  • Does it project forward, or only record what happened? Most personal finance software does the second.
  • If it projects, what tax does it apply — one flat rate, or the real bands for the right year and country?
  • Does it do inheritance tax at all, and for every year of the plan or only today?
  • For a firm: how many separate products is this, who connects them, and where does the Consumer Duty evidence come from?

Our answers are all on this site: in the edition comparison, the planning page and how it is built. There is also a feature-by-feature comparison with Microsoft Money. We can write that one because the product has not changed since 2009, and because we read its files.
